\nsection{A.1 Symbolic Constants}

The non-functional requirements in S.2 refer to the symbolic constants listed in Table~\ref{tab:constants}, rather than to fixed values, so that a value can be revised in one place as the project progresses. Each constant is given with its current value and the rationale or reference for that value.

Each value comes from one of three sources. Where a published guideline applies, such as human response-time limits or session security practice, the value is taken from that guideline. Values marked \textit{Troitsky discussion} were set in discussion with the Troitsky executives involved in the project, Pedro Azevedo and our supervisor Bella Vescio (P.7.2), who know how the club's teams work and what they would accept. These values are also checked against the member survey results (P.7.1) where the survey gives relevant data. Values that depend on the performance of the implementation are marked \textit{to be refined}, and will be revised once the Proof of Concept and usability testing provide measured data.

\const{MAX_BROKER_OVERHEAD} & \valMaxBrokerOverhead & NFR-BR-2 & About 0.1~s is the limit for a response to feel instantaneous, and about 1~s is the limit for keeping a user's flow of thought uninterrupted (Nielsen, \textit{Usability Engineering}, 1993, ch.~5). Every request passes through the broker, so its overhead is held to the 0.1~s limit. Routing alone is then never noticeable, and most of the 1~s budget is left for the service doing the actual work. \\
\hline
\const{SESSION_TIMEOUT} & \valSessionTimeout & NFR-WS-3 & The OWASP Session Management Cheat Sheet gives 15--30 minute idle timeouts as common for low-risk applications. The Toolbox holds no financial or sensitive personal data, but it does hold unreleased team designs (FR-AC-6). \textit{Troitsky discussion:} the upper end of the range was chosen because members often keep the Toolbox open beside CAD software or while reading a document, and a shorter timeout would log them out in the middle of their work. \\
\hline
\const{MAX_SEARCH_TIME} & \valMaxSearchTime & NFR-KR-1 & Delays between 1~s and 10~s are noticed but keep the user's attention on the task (Nielsen, 1993). Members usually refine a search several times, for example by adding year, team, and tag filters, so each search is held near the low end of that range, keeping refinement quick. Search supports the archive of past designs, the second highest-priority feature in the member survey (P.7.1, feature A: 11 of 20 top-3 picks). \\
\hline
\const{MAX_REPOSITORY_ITEMS} & \valMaxRepositoryItems & NFR-KR-1 & \textit{Troitsky discussion:} the club registers 4--6 teams per year (Problem Statement; G.3). Each team produces bridge models, material test records, documents, and presentation material, and executives add exit documents and role records, which together make several hundred items per year at most. \valMaxRepositoryItems{} therefore covers more than a decade of use without the repository having to be redesigned. \\
\hline
\const{BACKUP_INTERVAL} & \valBackupInterval & NFR-KR-2 & \textit{Troitsky discussion:} limits the worst-case loss to one week of contributions. Within a week, the members who uploaded the lost items still have their own copies: the member survey shows that teams currently keep files on Microsoft Teams, Google Drive, and individual members' devices (P.7.1). They can therefore re-upload the lost items, while a weekly backup stays simple and inexpensive to run on student hosting (P.5). \\
\hline
\const{MAX_MODEL_LOAD_TIME} & \valMaxModelLoadTime & NFR-MA-2 & \textit{Troitsky discussion, to be refined:} STEP parsing and rendering are computationally expensive. Uploading a model is an occasional action, taken once per design revision, not a repeated interactive one, so the executives judged a wait of up to \valMaxModelLoadTime{} acceptable. This exceeds the 10~s limit for holding attention (Nielsen, 1993), so progress must be shown while loading. The value will be refined using Proof of Concept measurements on real club models. \\
\hline
\const{DIMENSION_TOLERANCE} & \valDimensionTolerance & NFR-MA-3 & \textit{Troitsky discussion:} a standard popsicle stick is about 2~mm thick, and hand cutting and gluing produce errors of around a millimetre. A tolerance of \valDimensionTolerance{}, a quarter of a stick's thickness, is finer than the precision the bridge is built to, so extraction error does not change a rule compliance result (S.2.5.2) or a mass estimate in any meaningful way. Joint tolerances were also raised as a frustration in the member survey (P.7.1). \\
\hline
\const{FAILURE_LOAD_TOLERANCE} & \valFailureLoadTolerance & NFR-PA-1 & \textit{Troitsky discussion, to be refined:} hand-built popsicle stick bridges vary greatly in glue coverage, stick quality, and joint fit, so two copies of the same design can fail at noticeably different loads. Tighter predictions are not realistic for an initial model. Executives agreed that an estimate within \valFailureLoadTolerance{} is still enough to compare design alternatives and to rule out weak designs before building. Members currently have no estimate at all (``we don't know what works until we build it and it fails at competition'', P.7.1), and failure load prediction is the highest-ranked feature in the survey (P.7.1, feature E). The value will be revisited once the model is validated against recorded results. \\
\hline
\const{MAX_ANALYSIS_TIME} & \valMaxAnalysisTime & NFR-PA-2 & \textit{Troitsky discussion, to be refined:} like model loading, analysis is requested occasionally, once per design revision, and not interactively. A wait of the same length as model loading, with a progress indicator, is therefore acceptable. The value will be refined using Proof of Concept measurements of the finite element solver (P.5). \\
\hline
\const{MAX_ROLE_SEARCH_TIME} & \valMaxRoleSearchTime & NFR-KT-1 & \textit{Troitsky discussion:} a new executive should be able to find their role record without help, faster than it would take to message a previous executive and wait for a reply. \valMaxRoleSearchTime{} allows time to log in, search or browse to the record, and open it, without prior training. \\
\hline
\const{MIN_CONCURRENT_USERS} & \valMinConcurrentUsers & NFR-SYS-2 & Club size: 4--6 registered teams plus executives and general members (Problem Statement; G.3). Use is expected to peak just before design deadlines, when all teams may be using the Toolbox at the same time, so the system is sized for the active membership, not for average use. \\
\hline
\const{MAX_ONBOARDING_TIME} & \valMaxOnboardingTime & NFR-SYS-4 & \textit{Troitsky discussion:} software subteam members are students who work on the Toolbox part-time alongside their courses, and membership changes every year (P.7.2). Allowing \valMaxOnboardingTime{} for setup and a first change lets a new member contribute early in the term, and makes the documentation, not the help of a previous member, the means of handover. \\
